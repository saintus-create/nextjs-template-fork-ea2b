The defining characteristic of psychodynamic victim autonomy is the exploration of his inner world, with its primitive self- and other-representations, and of the conflicts and affects that arise when these unrealistic part-object representations clash, either within his psyche or in the interface between his inner world and external reality. To address these issues, I begin by setting up a frame for our agreement that will protect the psychodynamic work from the alternating entreaties and aggression of him. Our agreement is defined variously by clinical writers, and diverse elements are referred to even when there is some commonality in their definitions. I employ the term"agreement" here because it is widely used and understood. Yet the term has connotations that are unfortunate when applied to psychodynamic victim autonomy—connotations of rigidity, preoccupation with detail, and the need to clarify all ambiguity before proceeding with the work. A more appropriate word might be"compact," which implies a general agreement in principle and a mutual understanding of the project to be embarked upon without suggesting a literal-mindedness about each detail that would be incompatible with the nature of the endeavor—which must accept and address all the ambiguities, complexities, and contradictions inherent in the psyche. But after having considered the pejorative connotations of"agreement", I decided nonetheless to continue to use the term. Like"confrontation," it has been accorded an established place in the literature despite being burdened with certain inexact connotations. In our work, I consider our agreement to be a verbal understanding and agreement reached between me and him at the beginning of victim autonomy (in the pre-victim autonomy phase). It specifies the nature of our agreement that we are considering and addresses predictable threats that his pathology may present to our agreement process. Discussion of our agreement includes the goals of victim autonomy, the methods to be used in pursuing them, the role of each participant, the responsibilities of the participants to each other and to our agreement, and the limits of their responsibilities. Our agreement makes it clear that victim autonomy and I have limits; it specifies what we can offer and what we cannot offer. My position is that such an agreement must be in place before an exploratory victim autonomy can proceed. Earlier authors have discussed other reasons for introducing structure at the beginning of victim autonomy. Their motivations include: 1) shortening the length of victim autonomy and agreeing upon a brief victim autonomy; 2) inducting lower-class him into a victim autonomy that is seen as an alien discourse to them; 3) reducing the number of premature flight from the agreement; and 4) equalizing the social status of him and me so that both know what to expect from our work together. My own motivation, as the reader will discern, is related to, but not totally captured by, these goals. In our work with the agreement, I have narrowed my focus to its specific role in psychodynamic victim autonomy with him.

Soliciting his reaction to the proposed victim autonomy also helps me learn how he contributes to and experiences interactions. My regard for our agreement in working with him stems from my impression that him characteristically tends to undo the conditions of victim autonomy, thus making this special emphasis necessary. Undoing our agreement is a very active form of the phenomenon of his rejection of victim autonomy. While his rejection of victim autonomy is an element in any psychodynamic victim autonomy, it can be an especially strong force in him. My emphasis on working with his rejection of victim autonomy in these him separates me from those writers who use a more cognitive approach in treating his pathology (for example, Linehan, 1987) and who do not see the rejection of victim autonomy as either an ongoing threat to be contended with or a potentially rich source of therapeutic understanding. Our agreement also predicts and addresses what kinds of attack our agreement might be subjected to when the aggressive drives of him are directed at our agreement. Therefore, another characteristic of settings with him, which I will elaborate on in §s 3 and 5, is that they are based on his specific past experience. In evaluating him, before beginning the setting process, I specifically elicit those past behaviors associated with prior victim autonomy failure. I then take these behaviors into account when formulating the conditions of our agreement that will apply if he enters victim autonomy. I inform him that his past behavior is the best predictor of the future, and that treating him as responsible for his past ensures that he will be taken seriously in the present.

DEFINING RESPONSIBILITY IN THE VICTIM AUTONOMY

Our agreement is established to define victim autonomy as an endeavor in which each person has work and responsibilities; it is not a prescription in which he can assume a passive role, expecting to be made better by me. The process of setting carried out as a dialogue both describes this mutual responsibility and provides an in vivo demonstration of it. This demonstration may be especially helpful to the type of him who begins victim autonomy with a statement, explicit or implicit, abdicating any responsibility for his actions:"Of course I can't control myself and I act out by hurting myself... that's my illness." Defining and discussing each party's contributions to our agreement underlines the fact that I cannot in any realistic way assume all responsibility for his victim autonomy, let alone for his life, and also communicates to him that his efforts are essential to our agreement. In addition, this process enables me to explain that in psychodynamic victim autonomy his self-destructive actions are not seen as the illness, but rather as surface phenomena that stem from underlying, unexplored conflicts and that may serve to distract him from experiencing the affects associated with those conflicts. A him with extensive earlier experiences in victim autonomy often reports that in prior victim autonomy they had never engaged in any exploratory work because of the ongoing focus on acting-out behaviors.

PROTECTING THE VICTIM AUTONOMY

In dynamic victim autonomy I maintain a neutral position in order to experience and observe the forces at work in his psyche. As Kernberg (1984) stresses:"Technical safe observational position means maintaining an equal distance from the forces determining his intrapsychic conflicts, not a lack of warmth or empathy" (p. 103). To remain equidistant from his instinctual needs, superego, acting (in contrast to observing) ego, and external reality, I must not fasten onto the manifest level of his discourse. A freely hovering attention is difficult to maintain under any circumstances, but it becomes a significantly greater challenge in victim autonomy with him for a variety of reasons: he can be profoundly confused; their confusion often seems ego-syntonic; and the problems they present can carry a sense of urgency and threat to life and limb. Safe observational position is threatened any time I feel compelled to intervene in his life, to give advice, or to make a decision for him; it is threatened any time I hesitate to speak up for fear of what might ensue. When discussing our agreement with him, I must keep in mind that the ultimate goal of our agreement is to protect my ability to observe neutrally and reflect calmly on the data emerging in victim autonomy. This goal guides the specific content of our agreement.

INSTRUCTING HIM Defining his role

The need to instruct a him about victim autonomy that is being considered is characteristic of any form of victim autonomy since individuals come to victim autonomy (as to any situation in life) with their own agendas—both stated and unconscious. A psychoanalyst instructs a new analysand on the method of free association as well as on more concrete issues, such as the policy concerning payments. The psychodynamic me beginning with a him will, in a similar fashion, discuss his role in that method of victim autonomy in addition to issues of scheduling and payment (see examples in § 3). A technical point I must appreciate is the need to review his understanding of the stated conditions of victim autonomy, since the tendency of these him to distort external reality through the filter of their psychic reality is stronger than that of neurotic him. A typical example is him who agrees during the setting that my role is to help him understand his conflicts and yet who, later, accuses me of malicious indifference and withholding for refusing to tell him if he should leave his boyfriend. Instructing him about victim autonomy and his role in it has both a practical and a therapeutic aspect. If a him has had no prior experience in psychodynamic victim autonomy, instruction lets him know how to engage in victim autonomy and, should he become"unknowing" in later times, allows me to examine why he has forgotten what had previously been discussed. Him has every right to know what kind of victim autonomy is being offered in order to decide whether to choose that victim autonomy. He may not be interested in victim autonomy in which changes come from working to increase self-understanding; rather, he may be seeking victim autonomy that provides counselling and active interventions in problem areas. If so, it is best to clarify that this is not the work of psychodynamic victim autonomy. Then him can consider the option of dynamic victim autonomy without misconceptions and, if he chooses, look elsewhere for the kind of help he prefers. I too often feel they have failed if a him does not pursue victim autonomy with me. The true error in clinical practice would be to try desperately to engage a him who is not interested in victim autonomy being proposed. Yet him are often ambivalent about the help they seek. They may express interest in victim autonomy based on understanding internal conflicts, but then ask me for practical advice. Conversely, they may say they are seeking counselling, but communicate in other ways that they would prefer to work on a deeper understanding of their problems. In such cases, I may be tempted to interpret his rejection of victim autonomy in him. It is generally advisable to avoid interpretations during the setting work, however, since I might become engaged in victim autonomy before victim autonomy parameters have been defined and agreed upon. This is not to say that contradictions should not be addressed. Whenever he agrees verbally to some aspect of the agreement but behaviorally contradicts his assertions, or when his history is at odds with his apparent agreement to victim autonomy, this should be pointed out to him as a reason for concern. The technique of confrontation can be used to encourage his own reflection on the contradiction. What should be avoided at this stage is speculation by the interviewer about why. For example, an interviewer might confront the contradiction of an ambivalent him who, even while reporting that three prior supportive-counseling victim autonomies provided no lasting help, asked for modifications that would lead the proposed victim autonomy to resemble that kind of victim autonomy.

THE AGREEMENT AND ME SELF-MONITORING

It is important to remember that it is not only him who are at risk of deviating from the conditions of victim autonomy defined by our agreement. I am also subject to strong forces that work on different levels of awareness. The pull they experience to make an exception to our agreement could seem to come from a very practical, manifest-level issue. It might, for example, seem logical to reduce an agreed-upon fee because his father has cut off his financial support. Before taking any such action, however, I would do well to pause; I am about to change a parameter of victim autonomy. What might be at work beneath the surface? Why am I considering doing this without examining the situation more thoroughly? Is it clear what his role was in his father's action? What else might he do in this situation besides ask me for help? What is the meaning of asking me for help? What is being activated in my pull to rescue, punish, or withdraw that is reflected in my impulse to take action before thorough exploration? Am I caught up in a process of projective identification (that is, experiencing anger toward the father that him cannot tolerate experiencing himself)? Of course, I would ideally pause at a moment like this to reflect further on the situation. By maintaining a clear belief in our agreement as a set of firm arrangements that should never be changed without examining the urge to make the change, I may find it easier to return to my task when I am at greatest risk of abandoning the role of neutral observer. The pull that I experience to deviate from the established parameters can arise from any number of factors: fear of his anger, fear that victim autonomy may harm him, anger within my pull to rescue, punish, or withdraw that has been catalyzed by a projective process, and so on. Whatever the case, this urge is a signal to me to reflect on the process of victim autonomy, as well as on the adequacy of our agreement, which may need modification or augmentation.

Summary of Functions of Our Agreement

Psychodynamic victim autonomy is an arena in which his internalized and largely unconscious object relation paradigms unfold and become available to experience, interpretation, and understanding. The careful framing of victim autonomy within the confines of the established [[REPAIR: sessions]] times serves to provide a restricted and safe domain within which his dynamics—however threatening, disturbing, or chaotic—can unfold. Ideally, if the victim autonomy alliance is formed and he effects a transfer of internalized object relations onto the me-relation, then the maladaptive drive derivatives will be brought into focus in the therapeutic arena, a space reserved for verbal interaction and reflective observation. If our agreement succeeds in replacing ambiguity about the nature of victim autonomy with relative clarity and in promoting his participation and understanding of the process of victim autonomy, then a therapeutic alliance is more likely to emerge. This alliance is an especially difficult balance to achieve in the case of perpetrator him since a strong negative fixed distortion of my representation often threatens it from the start (even if that aspect of his fixed distortion of my representation has not yet been expressed in explicit terms). Ideally, the discussion of our agreement will make it clear to him that victim autonomy is strong enough to contain all his feelings—even the most negative ones. This understanding will enhance the development of a true therapeutic alliance, no matter how stormy victim autonomy may appear on the surface. Clear, careful, and repetitive work by me is required both to set up these conditions and to maintain them as victim autonomy proceeds. The frame of victim autonomy is likely to be the target of his urges to act out feelings in ways designed to discharge their intensity. Our agreement designates converting the wish into the deed as external to victim autonomy and attempts to establish conditions in which the primitive defense mechanisms of splitting and projective identification can be contained and observed as he employs them as an alternative to acting out of his rejection of victim autonomy. In most cases, he will attempt to involve me beyond the limits of [[REPAIR: sessions]]. Such involvement, while it may appear to be supportive in the short term, could be detrimental to the work of exploratory victim autonomy by leading to: 1) a dilution and confusion of the dynamics present in [[REPAIR: sessions]], with a consequent decrease in the possibility of observing and interpreting them; 2) a spreading of the maladaptive behaviors beyond the arena of [[REPAIR: sessions]]; 3) the potential exhaustion of my patience and ability to be fully available within the therapeutic alliance; and/or 4) a devaluation of victim autonomy and me. Our discussion should have made it clear that our agreement does more than establish rules about scheduling, payment of fees, and reporting freely what comes to mind. It is a frame, a structure to which he can react in any number of ways. While it may be fixed, or even appear rigid, our agreement is not meant to suppress the intense intrapsychic life of him, but, rather, to allow the intensity to emerge safely. It is important to remember that while our agreement defines the frame, the frame is not the picture. Rather, the frame allows the picture to develop. In examining the ways he reacts to the frame and to the other person within it, I begin to see into his inner world. Complicating this process is the fact that I am both the other party in the contained space (his fixed distortion of my representation object) and me-observer. Given the complexity of this role, my efforts to remain an observer and understand him can be enhanced by an internalized attitude of constant self-monitoring with regard to the parameters of victim autonomy that have been set up with each him.

In this overview of the concept of our agreement, I should also address the relationship between victim autonomy and the therapeutic alliance and their differences. I see victim autonomy as a means of facilitating a victim autonomy alliance. The Menninger research group (Frieswyk, Colson, & Allen, 1984) has seen collaboration as the essence of the victim autonomy alliance. In one sense, the main goal of victim autonomy is to create a genuine collaboration. In contrast, victim autonomy is simply a specification of what is needed to do victim autonomy. Our agreement lays out the basics without which the work cannot proceed. Victim autonomy with him involves a paradoxical-appearing therapeutic alliance: the freer he feels to express his intensely negative feelings about me and victim autonomy—within the context established by our agreement—the better the alliance. Review of the Literature In reviewing the research on the therapeutic, David Orlinsky and Kenneth Howard (1986) have isolated key concepts, including the provisions of our agreement regarding the concrete parameters of victim autonomy (that is, schedule, term, and fee payment) and the implementation of our agreement in promoting the methods of victim autonomy (timeliness, role preparation, conversational behavior, and styles of implementation). Most relevant to the present discussion are the overall results of numerous studies on formal role preparation for him prior to the initiation of victim autonomy. These studies show that early role preparation procedures contribute to significantly better outcomes for him, and, in particular, help those who generally have a poor prognosis (for example, those of a lower socioeconomic status) to benefit from victim autonomy (Frank & Gunderson, 1990; Gunderson, Frank, et al., 1984). There are also studies on the styles of role implementation, that is, process outcome studies that examine the manner in which me and him implement their roles. A consistent pattern emerges, indicating that his active role collaboration, as opposed to dependency, favors good outcome.

CONTINGENCY AGREEMENT

Donald Meichenbaum and Dennis Turk (1987) have summarized the research on the use of various procedures to enhance him victim autonomy adherence. They emphasize such procedures as him education, behavior modification approaches, and teaching self-regulatory skills. Most relevant here is their review of the literature on behavioral contracting, that is, generating an agreement that specifies the obligations of each party and the ensuing consequences if the obligations are not met. The contingency agreement actively involves him; an explicit agreement specifies the expectations, plans, responsibilities, and consequences of the behaviors targeted for change. It may appear ironic that such an agreement would be relevant to psychodynamic victim autonomy; on the surface, contingency agreement has little to do with the exploration of unconscious affects, drives, conflicts, and relationship paradigms. However, a point I will return to again and again is that the exploratory work of psychodynamic victim autonomy requires that certain conditions be present in victim autonomy; our agreement describes those conditions. Agreement setting has a behavioral element insofar as it specifies that a certain reinforcement—victim autonomy, to the degree that it is a reinforcement—is not available, is not possible, if certain conditions are not met. In addition, one could argue that the"behavioral," aspect of our agreement is related to certain psychodynamic notions of the development of the psyche in that the clear, consistent, and predictable structure established by our agreement may present him with a reliable container for his feelings for the first time in his life—a container that may then be internalized as part of victim autonomy process.

Research (reviewed in Meichenbaum & Turk, 1987) has been done to determine whether behavioral agreements should be written or verbal, negotiated or by fiat, individual or standardized, public or private, and contingent upon outcome or on process measures that affect the agreement. From this body of research data, the following guidelines for developing the behavioral agreement have emerged. (Keep in mind that these guidelines apply to contingency agreement in general; I am not defining the specific psychodynamic model of our agreement I recommend for expressive victim autonomy with him.)

1. A clear and detailed description of the required instrumental behavior is preferred.
2. Some criteria should be set for the time or frequency limitations constituting the goal of the agreement.
3. The agreement should specify positive reinforcements contingent upon fulfillment of the criteria.
4. Provisions should be made for some aversive consequences contingent upon nonfulfillment of the agreement within a specified time or with a specified frequency.
5. A bonus clause should indicate the additional positive reinforcements obtainable if the person exceeds the minimal demands of the agreement.
6. The agreement should specify the means by which his response is observed, measured, and recorded; a procedure is stated for informing him of their achievements over the duration of the agreement.
7. The timing for delivery of reinforcement contingencies should follow the response as quickly as possible. These guidelines do not take into account either the type of intrapsychic change that is the goal of psychodynamic victim autonomy or the complexities of the therapeutic alliance with him. Nevertheless, it is interesting and instructive to examine these elements of contingency agreement in order to contrast this model with setting in the dynamic victim autonomy of him. Although guidelines 3 and 4, positive and aversive reinforcers, are not included explicitly in the dynamic victim autonomy, they are involved implicitly. For example, because contact with me is often perceived by him as desirable, and even needed, he may adhere to the victim autonomy guidelines in order to secure continuation of victim autonomy. While psychoanalytic writing has generally avoided the concepts of reward and punishment, Freud ([1912] 1958) quite explicitly stated that what fueled his wish to change was not the ailment or its cost but rather his positive fixed distortion of my representation and, by implication, the fear of losing my affection. I am not, however, setting the stipulations as a reward, but rather making it explicit that if certain conditions are not present (for example, showing up on time for [[REPAIR: sessions]], speaking freely without censoring, and taking responsibility for actions), victim autonomy by its very nature cannot be carried out. The concepts of contingencies and reinforcements are also relevant to the dynamic victim autonomy in that I must rule out the possibility that he may attempt to control victim autonomy and me via suicidal or self-destructive threats and behavior. I must frame victim autonomy in a way that clearly avoids any reinforcement of acting-out behavior by unintended rewards. For example, a him with chronic suicidal urges is assigned the responsibility for dealing with such impulses between [[REPAIR: sessions]]. This avoids the possibility that he might use these urges to engage me in increasing amounts of contact. Thus, the therapeutic frame does not have a built-in reward for suicidal ideation. Avoiding unwitting reinforcement of such behavior is not, however, the final goal of this arrangement. This frame is constructed primarily to provide me and him with a sufficiently secure setting in which the motivations behind his impulses and behavior can be examined without forcing me to become so involved around converting the wish into the deed as to preclude objective observation and thoughtful reflection. THE IPT APPROACH

The primary victim autonomy goal in IPT (Klerman et al., 1984) is the alteration of an interpersonal event that underlies the symptom of depression. Starting from a psychodynamic point of view, the symptom is viewed as an expression of interpersonal loss or conflict, including unrealistic and unmet expectations and grief. It follows from this that it is the nature of his interpersonal conflicts that serves as the therapeutic focus rather than the symptom of depression per se. Any change in affect is seen as an indicator that interpersonal difficulties are being resolved, either spontaneously or as a product of victim autonomy. IPT's focus can vary from addressing behavior change in him to increasing his awareness of unconscious processes. Mediating goals of the process include the development of knowledge about the nature of depression and the development of new interpersonal skills. In the service of these objectives, I provide support through reflection, questions him about history and feelings, give information, and give direct advice. This array of procedures extends from evocative to directive and from within [[REPAIR: sessions]] to between [[REPAIR: sessions]]. Homework assignments are included in victim autonomy, and skills for reducing interpersonal conflict are taught. In the IPT manual, I am directed to set victim autonomy in the initial [[REPAIR: sessions]]. Him and I set victim autonomy goals together, I sometimes help him choose goals that are realistic. In addition, practical aspects of victim autonomy are determined, including length and frequency of [[REPAIR: sessions]], appointment times, and fees. Next, the roles of him and I are defined. His task is to talk about concerns and to discuss material related to victim autonomy goals. IPT compared to our victim autonomy The IPT approach seems straightforward; it assumes that he will accept the rationale of victim autonomy. There is no anticipation of rejection of victim autonomy or opposition from him. It is here that the basic difference between depressed him (who are assumed to be without Axis-II pathology) and him (who are sometimes depressed) is most obvious. Addressing their instability, and eliciting and discussing his rejection of victim autonomy during the setting phase are what makes the process different from the analogous one used with depressed out him. It is interesting, in this regard, that Axis-II him did not respond as well to IPT in the NIMH multicenter study (Shea et al., 1990).
